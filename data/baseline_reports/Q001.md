**Yes, AI coding assistants can reduce software-development task completion time, but the effect is not universal.** Randomized studies show substantial time savings in some settings and a measurable slowdown in another. The evidence supports benefits that depend on the task, developer, and tool—not a single general percentage improvement. [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944) [[3]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)

| Study and setting | Empirical result | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Peng et al. (2023):** 95 professional programmers randomized to use GitHub Copilot or work without it on a JavaScript HTTP-server task. | Among participants who finished, average completion time was **71 versus 161 minutes: a 55.8% reduction** (95% confidence interval: 21–89%). Completion required passing automated tests. [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) | One standardized task; the time estimate conditions on completion. Broader code quality and long-term maintenance were not evaluated. |
| **Paradis et al. (2024):** 96 Google engineers randomized to receive AI features for an enterprise coding task. | Mean completion times were **96 versus 114 minutes**. The preferred adjusted estimate was approximately **21% less time**, but its confidence interval included no effect (*p*=0.086); the unadjusted comparison was statistically significant. [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944) | A bounded experimental task using internal Google tools, limiting generalization. |
| **Becker et al., METR (2025):** 16 experienced open-source developers completed 246 real tasks in repositories they knew well; tasks were randomized to allow or prohibit AI. | AI access **increased completion time by 19%**; the reported confidence interval was approximately 2–39% longer. Developers primarily used Cursor with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. [[3]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089) [[5]](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) | A small, specialized developer sample and a particular generation of tools. |

Larger workplace experiments provide supporting—but indirect—evidence. Cui et al. pooled randomized trials involving **4,867 developers** at Microsoft, Accenture, and another large company. Their preferred estimate was **26.08% more completed tasks per week** among developers induced to adopt Copilot (standard error: 10.3 percentage points). This measures throughput, so it should not be interpreted as a 26% reduction in individual task completion time. Individual experiments were noisy, and adoption was incomplete. [[4]](https://demirermert.github.io/Papers/Demirer_AI_productivity.pdf)

More recent evidence remains uncertain. In February 2026, METR reported follow-up estimates suggesting **18% less time for returning developers and 4% less for newly recruited developers**, but both confidence intervals included no effect. METR judged these estimates unreliable because of participant and task selection, alongside difficulties measuring time when developers used concurrent agents. The follow-up therefore suggests possible improvement without establishing its magnitude. [[5]](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

The defensible interpretation is that **AI assistance can materially shorten particular coding tasks, while evidence does not establish a universal reduction in development time or total project delivery time**. The studies differ too much in tasks, participants, tools, and outcome measures to justify averaging their headline percentages. [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944) [[3]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089) [[4]](https://demirermert.github.io/Papers/Demirer_AI_productivity.pdf)
